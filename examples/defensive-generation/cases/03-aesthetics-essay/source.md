# Constructed materials: The Rehearsal Room

All artworks, speakers, quotations, observations, and institutional details below were invented for this test. They do not describe a real exhibition or paraphrase a published aesthetic theory. The letters R and S designate fictional theorists.

## Exhibition description

The Rehearsal Room contains two identical wooden doors set in parallel partitions. Each door opens into a narrow, empty space and then closes automatically. Visitors may enter either space. A light above each door turns green when entry is possible. The doors are quiet, unpainted, and slightly heavier than an ordinary interior door.

During the first week, the green lights respond to a fixed sequence: the left door is available for thirty seconds, then the right door for thirty seconds. A small sign accurately explains this mechanism. During the second week, the lights respond to movements recorded from the previous day's visitors. Each present visitor therefore encounters a sequence partly produced by earlier visitors' approaches, hesitations, and departures. The sign explains this mechanism too. No individual visitor can be identified from the sequence. In the third week, the first week's fixed sequence returns, but the second week's sign is accidentally left in place for one afternoon.

In all three weeks, staff can hold either door open for someone who cannot comfortably move it. Staff do so when asked, but this is not mentioned on the sign. The artist calls this assistance “part of keeping the work available.”

## Artist's statement

“I wanted an ordinary movement to acquire a past. The visitor need not discover whose past it is. A door can be encountered as a resistant thing, a permission, or the delayed consequence of somebody else's movement. The work does not instruct visitors which of these to feel. I chose doors because people normally pass through them without asking what makes passage possible.”

## Fictional theoretical fragments

**R:** “An account of an object's production can redirect attention without becoming an aesthetically relevant property. A visitor might inspect a chair more carefully after learning who made it, yet admire precisely what was visible before. To establish the relevance of production, one must explain what the new information allows the visitor to perceive or appreciate. An alteration in attention alone does not settle this question.”

**S:** “Some works are encountered through relations that cannot be read from their surfaces. A recording presented as a reply differs from the same sounds presented as an accident. This does not make every belief about a work part of the work. The relation must belong to its organization, and an interpretation must be answerable to whether that relation actually obtains.”

## Constructed visitor records

The following are selected observations, not a representative survey. The labels identify individual visits, not stable kinds of audience.

- **Visit A, week one:** A visitor reads the sign, waits through two cycles, and says, “Once I had the rhythm, I stopped paying attention.” The visitor subsequently times a passage without looking at either light.
- **Visit B, week two:** A visitor reads the sign and approaches the left door three times without entering. “I kept wondering whether someone yesterday had done this too. Then I realized I might be arranging someone else's wait tomorrow.” The visitor asks whether refusing to enter is recorded. Staff answer that approaches and departures are both recorded.
- **Visit C, week two:** A visitor does not read the sign. After several failed attempts to catch a green light, the visitor calls the work “a bad entrance system.” Staff explain the recorded movements. The visitor responds, “That explains its schedule. It doesn't make waiting enjoyable.”
- **Visit D, third-week mistaken-sign afternoon:** A visitor reads the incorrect sign and says, “It feels as though strangers are making room for me.” The visitor's movements resemble those of Visit B. After being told about the incorrect sign, the visitor says, “The feeling happened, but I no longer think this door was doing what I admired.”
- **Visit E, week two:** A visitor cannot comfortably move the heavy door. Staff hold it open. The visitor says, “I experienced being welcomed by that person. I hardly noticed what the light was doing.” The artist later describes this as “another relation through which entry becomes possible.”

## A material change proposed by the venue

The venue proposes replacing both doors with lightweight doors, displaying an invitation to ask for assistance, and projecting an animated diagram of the previous day's recorded movements. The artist worries that the diagram will turn an uncertain encounter into a demonstration. Staff argue that visitors should not have to struggle physically before they can attend to the work.
