# Passage with a Past

A visitor to The Rehearsal Room can appreciate how an apparently private movement becomes dependent on movements elsewhere in time. In week two, approaching, waiting, and passing through a door give bodily form to that dependence. The information about previous visitors matters aesthetically when it changes the organization someone can follow in these actions. I argue that the work gives hesitation a double temporal role: it responds to an arrangement inherited from strangers while becoming material for another arrangement. Appreciation concerns how that double role takes shape within the practical business of attempting passage.

The fixed sequence of week one establishes a simpler relation between movement and timing. In Visit A, the visitor learns the alternation so thoroughly that passage becomes possible without checking the lights. Thirty seconds becomes an anticipated interval rather than a succession of visual instructions. The eventual inattention records successful bodily adjustment, suggesting that familiarity has diminished this visitor's interest. Predictability gives passage a rhythm the visitor can acquire and use. Week two changes the object of such adjustment: the sequence partly results from earlier approaches, hesitations, and departures. R rightly demands more than the claim that knowing this makes visitors attentive. Someone could learn the mechanism while continuing to appreciate only the rhythm available in week one. What requires explanation is how recorded movements change the organization of the present action.

Visit B approaches the left door three times without entering, then asks whether refusal is recorded. The answer reveals consequences of these approaches beyond reaching the empty space. Each can now be understood both as a response to received opportunities and as a contribution to subsequent ones. A visitor attending to this relation can appreciate the tension within withholding entry: the decision to stop still continues through the recording into another day's sequence. Repeated approaches make that tension available in a small, observable pattern. The same movement that negotiates an inherited condition also helps create conditions for later visitors. The aesthetic interest lies in how economically the work gives this extended relation a bodily shape. A hesitation ordinarily dismissed as an unsuccessful preliminary becomes the place where receiving and producing a sequence coincide. Its significance changes the scale of what someone can attend to in the action: the pause belongs to an immediate decision and to a longer arrangement passing through it.

This account gives R's distinction a practical consequence. Appreciating the organization involves attending to how approach and withdrawal hold those temporal positions together; merely explaining why the light changes leaves that work of attention undone. Visit C understands the cause and still calls waiting unenjoyable. The information has not evidently changed what this visitor finds worthwhile in the encounter. The interruptions could be judged effective because they make one's limited control palpable through repeated adjustment, or ineffective because they remain a succession of imposed delays. These judgments concern the experienced organization of the relation, and can differ even when both acknowledge the mechanism. Visit B's curiosity identifies something available for appreciation; it does not decide how successfully every encounter realizes it.

The mistaken-sign afternoon tests whether this appreciation can survive the loss of its supposed relation. S makes actual organization decisive: recorded activity must really contribute to the conditions of entry. Visit D feels that strangers are making room, but the doors follow a fixed cycle. After correction, the visitor distinguishes having felt something from the door doing what was admired. The sign has organized felt passage without correctly describing its cause. The emotional event remains, while the claim that this passage receives another visitor's action loses its object. Even an accurate second-week sign would not establish generosity. Earlier visitors' departures may help shape an available interval without having been intended to welcome anyone. The relation I have described is therefore an anonymous transmission of consequences. Its interest comes from a current action receiving and extending those consequences, including effects that anticipate none of this visitor's needs.

Actual welcome introduces a different relation within passage. In Visit E, staff hold the door open and the visitor experiences welcome from that person. Unlike D's imagined generosity, this feeling has an observed action supporting it. R's distinction still matters: gratitude can remain directed toward the helper. Yet the assistance can also enter attention to how passage is organized. Someone holding the door adjusts its availability to a particular body, bringing a responsive action into the timed arrangement. A visitor could appreciate the change from coordinating alone with a sequence to moving through an opportunity sustained by another person. This develops the original account: relations extending beyond the visitor can become perceptible both through recorded consequences and through another person's present participation. E's report emphasizes the person and scarcely notices the lights, so it records the welcome without establishing that further appreciation.

The venue's proposal would alter these possibilities unevenly. Lighter doors would change resistance without necessarily removing the temporal relation between visitors. Their weight makes effort salient, but the analysis above depends on approaching and hesitating within an inherited sequence; those actions can continue with lighter doors. An invitation to request assistance would make its availability something visitors can anticipate. The diagram changes how the past is encountered: what presently affects movement through the sequence would also appear as an image open to inspection. It could help visitors return to their own attempts with a clearer grasp of the double role described above. It could also make inspecting the mechanism absorb the attention previously given to negotiating passage. The artist's concern identifies a possible shift between those activities. Explanation can enter the encounter productively when it enables someone to follow more in the movement they are making.

The Rehearsal Room makes ordinary passage appreciable by giving its small decisions a temporal reach. The visitor receives an anonymous arrangement, adjusts to it, and leaves movements that can help arrange another person's encounter. Hesitation gains form as both an interruption of entry and a continuation of the work across visits. Accurate information matters because that continuation is part of what is being appreciated. Assistance makes another aspect of dependence available through responsive action. Together, these possibilities show how the work enlarges an apparently private movement: the visitor can follow other lives entering its timing and their own action becoming consequential beyond its present occasion.
