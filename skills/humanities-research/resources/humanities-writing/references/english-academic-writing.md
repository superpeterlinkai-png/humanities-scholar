# English humanities writing

Use for English drafting, editing, or review. Apply only the sections relevant to
the task; a review request calls for findings rather than an automatic rewrite.
The examples are illustrative wording, not sourced scholarly claims.

## Register and genre

Preserve the draft's established English variety, terminology, citation system,
and authorial stance. Do not choose British or American usage solely from the
author's institution. Match a course essay, book review, historical account,
philosophical argument, or proposal to its actual audience and purpose.

Natural academic English can retain difficult concepts, long sentences, and
carefully qualified claims. Improve syntax that obstructs the argument without
replacing precise vocabulary with a more familiar but different concept.

## Sentence-level distinctions that affect meaning

- Check the agent and referents of “it,” “this,” and “they.” Name an antecedent only
  when the source or context identifies it; grammatical clarity cannot supply an
  unknown historical actor or resolve an unsettled interpretation.
- Articles and quantifiers can change scope: “a community,” “the community,” and
  “all communities” make different commitments. Do not generalize a bounded claim
  while correcting grammar or smoothing a plural.
- Preserve modal and negative scope. “Does not necessarily imply” does not mean
  “does not imply”; “fails to establish” does not mean “disproves.”
- Use tense according to function. A text's argument may be discussed in the
  present while its composition and historical events take past tense. Do not
  change every verb to one tense for surface consistency.
- Clarify overloaded clauses, nominalizations, and stacked prepositional phrases
  where useful. Passive voice can appropriately foreground an object or process;
  active voice helps when agency is known and relevant. Neither is a universal rule.
- Check parallelism for shared grammatical and logical scope. Do not make unlike
  positions seem equivalent just to produce balanced syntax.

## Paragraph movement and voice

Let connected analysis develop within a paragraph, with breaks at consequential
changes in the question, evidence relation, or direction of reasoning. Several
authors and analytic moves can share a paragraph; length follows their development.
Read adjacent paragraphs together for how later reasoning uses earlier findings.
Repeated author-summary-question or author-summary-caution cycles can continually
restart the inquiry. Develop the unanswered question and state the judgment the
evidence supports. Varying openings or joining paragraphs alone cannot repair a
missing inference. Keep language-only edits within scope and identify any substantive
gap that needs further work. Preserve historical development and source-led openings
where they serve the inquiry.

Use transitions when they express a real relation. “Therefore” needs an inference;
“by contrast” needs a contrast. Replacing a Chinese connective word for word can
misstate the argument. Repair the relation, not just the connective.

Retain “I” or “we” when justified by the author's voice and genre. Translating
“本文” does not authorize inventing a research team or a collective fieldwork
experience. “This paper argues” and a first-person formulation are choices to
make from context, not a rule to apply everywhere.

## Evidence and expression

Keep attribution and evidential strength visible. “Suggests,” “establishes,” and
“demonstrates” are not stylistic synonyms. A fluent paraphrase should preserve who
holds a position, its conditions, and its limits. Do not add scholarship to make
a language edit sound authoritative.

Treat stock wording and repeated emphases as editing clues. Keep a word such as
“complex,” “genuinely,” or “represents” when its precise meaning is necessary.
Avoid substituting a different unsupported claim when removing an inflated phrase.

Preserve direct quotations during same-language editing. Check quotation punctuation
against the established variety or house style without silently changing source
wording. For a requested translation, follow the translation guide.

If a hard word limit applies, count the requested material under the specified
convention; abstract, notes, and bibliography exclusions depend on the actual rule.
Ordinary shortening needs no arbitrary percentage or fixed sentence-length target.

Check changed passages for meaning, scope, terms, and citation integrity. Explain
consequential choices briefly; do not append a grammar lesson to every local edit.
