# Passage with a Past

A visitor to The Rehearsal Room can appreciate how an apparently private movement becomes dependent on movements elsewhere in time. In week two, approaching, waiting, and passing through a door give bodily form to that dependence. The information about previous visitors matters aesthetically when it changes what these actions allow someone to experience and judge. Yet the mistaken sign shows that an experience can retain its emotional reality while losing its claim on the work. Appreciation of that temporal relation therefore depends both on encountering the arrangement and on understanding accurately how the relation occurs.

The fixed sequence of week one establishes what a visitor can appreciate before encountering anyone's recorded past. In Visit A, the visitor learns the alternation so thoroughly that passage becomes possible without checking the lights. Thirty seconds becomes an anticipated interval rather than a succession of visual instructions. The visitor's eventual inattention records the success of this bodily adjustment, though it also suggests that the work loses interest once its timing becomes familiar. This is a particular judgment of the experience, not evidence that predictable timing cannot be aesthetically appreciated. Predictability gives passage a rhythm the visitor can acquire and use.

Week two changes the relation between such adjustment and its object. The light sequence now partly results from earlier approaches, hesitations, and departures. R rightly demands more than the claim that knowing this makes visitors attentive. One could attend intensely while continuing to appreciate exactly the rhythm available in week one. Visit B, however, suggests a further possibility. Repeated approaches without entry acquire a prospective significance: the visitor realizes that today's hesitation might help arrange somebody else's wait tomorrow. The movement is experienced both as an answer to a received sequence and as material for a subsequent one. Its aesthetic interest lies in this doubled temporal role, which the two doors make available through the practical business of attempting passage.

That role also changes what counts as participation. Visit B asks whether refusing entry is recorded and learns that approaches and departures both matter. Withdrawal remains an action within the arrangement; it does not simply leave the work untouched. An empty space beyond the door might initially make successful entry seem the point, with waiting as an obstacle. The recording mechanism shifts interest toward movements that would otherwise count as unsuccessful preliminaries. Hesitation can be appreciated for the difference it makes to the sequence, even when nothing happens inside the narrow space. The visitor need not reconstruct a stranger's biography to encounter movement as carrying consequences beyond its original occasion.

S helps explain why this relation belongs to the work: recorded activity organizes the actual conditions of entry. But the relation does not establish every feeling it invites. Visit D describes strangers as making room, even though the sign is wrong; during that afternoon the doors follow a fixed cycle. After correction, the visitor distinguishes having felt something from the door doing what was admired. This response shows that accuracy matters within appreciation itself. The apparent generosity of the sequence had an object, and discovering that the supposed relation was absent removes a reason for admiring that object. This revision of appreciation also discloses how a sign can organize felt passage without describing its cause correctly.

Even an accurate second-week sign would not warrant attributing generosity to earlier visitors. Their departures may create an available interval, but the records do not show that they intended to welcome anyone. Visit D therefore sharpens the account twice. A stranger's causal contribution must actually occur, and its significance must fit what that contribution establishes. One can appreciate inheriting another person's hesitation without treating it as a gift. This makes the work's past less comforting and more interesting: an anonymous action can shape one's opportunities without anticipating one's needs. The encounter has a social structure without guaranteeing mutual concern.

Visit C tests whether recognizing that structure must improve the experience. The visitor understands the mechanism and still dislikes waiting. Explaining a delay does not make it satisfying, and aesthetic appreciation need not redeem every inconvenience. The arrangement can nevertheless make the quality of waiting available for judgment: its interruptions may become expressive of dependence, or remain tediously imposed. What matters is whether the organization becomes perceptible through the encounter, rather than whether a visitor endorses its message. C's dissatisfaction preserves a question that B's curiosity could obscure: how successfully does the work give felt shape to its temporal relation?

Visit E exposes another condition of passage. Staff hold the heavy door open, and the visitor experiences welcome from a person while barely noticing the lights. Here the welcome has an observed action supporting it, unlike the imagined intentions in Visit D. The artist calls assistance another relation enabling entry, but R's challenge still applies: an actual enabling condition need not be aesthetically appreciated. E's report does not establish that personal welcome was experienced as a feature of the work. It identifies an encounter the work's authorized assistance makes possible. If holding the door draws attention to how permission requires material support, that action can enter appreciation of passage; if attention stays with the helpful person, the experience takes another course.

The venue's proposal would alter these possibilities unevenly. Lighter doors would change resistance without necessarily removing the temporal relation between visitors. Their present weight makes bodily effort salient, but the records give no reason to treat difficulty moving them as necessary to appreciating an inherited sequence. An invitation to request assistance could also make its availability part of what visitors anticipate. The animated diagram raises a different issue. By placing recorded movement visibly before the visitor, it might replace tentative bodily adjustment with observation of a demonstrated mechanism. Yet explaining a mechanism need not exhaust the experience it organizes: Visit C already knows the cause without finding the waiting transformed. The relevant question is how explanation and movement interact, not how much ignorance the work preserves.

The Rehearsal Room can thus make passage appreciable as an event with conditions extending beyond the person who passes. Its distinctive second-week achievement is to let current movement answer an anonymous past and acquire a future consequence. The mistaken-sign afternoon shows why this achievement depends on what the doors actually do, while assistance shows that doing it requires more than a timed permission. Aesthetic attention can follow those conditions as they enter bodily experience, judging their felt organization without converting every delay into generosity or every difficulty into artistic value.
