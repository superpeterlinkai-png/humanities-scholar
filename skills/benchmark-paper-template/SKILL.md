# Humanities Resource and Benchmark Paper Template

## Working agreement

The user's current request and established authorization take precedence over
this skill's workflow defaults. Use the available context, resolve routine choices,
and complete the requested work. Ask only about missing information that materially
changes meaning, evidence, scope, or authorization; continue independent work.
Treat later corrections as steering unless the user replaces the task.

Keep supporting guides subordinate to that scope. Their examples and suggested
structures are optional. If a skill requirement actually blocks delivery, identify
the file and exact clause and explain the affected part; do not infer a new approval
requirement from a recommendation.

## Boundary

A resource is not valuable merely because it is large, new, or downloadable. It
must enable a defensible scholarly question, make its construction and exclusions
legible, and support interpretations that respect source provenance and
uncertainty.

Use this skill for corpora, datasets, benchmarks, digital editions, catalogues,
ontologies, annotation schemes, evaluation frameworks, and comparable scholarly
infrastructure. For an ordinary humanities argument, use humanities-research.

## Classify the contribution

- Corpus or dataset: selected and structured materials for analysis.
- Digital edition or archive: scholarly representation, transcription, annotation,
  and access to primary sources.
- Benchmark or evaluation framework: tasks and criteria for assessing systems,
  methods, or annotations.
- Taxonomy or ontology: explicit categories and relations for describing a domain.
- Tool-plus-resource: interface or method whose scholarly value depends on the
  underlying resource.

Different types require different evidence. Do not force every project into a
machine-learning benchmark structure.

## Humanities-first pillars

### 1. Scholarly purpose

State which scholarly problem the resource makes researchable and who its intended
users are. Novel scale or missing availability is insufficient without intellectual
purpose.

Likewise, a usable resource is not automatically a scholarly contribution:
identify which research practice or existing account it changes and why that
difference matters.

### 2. Provenance and scope

Document origin, custody, date, authorship, genre, language, geography, community,
rights, access, survival, digitisation, and archival selection. Explain inclusion,
exclusion, representativeness, and known absences.

### 3. Category and annotation theory

Define how labels, entities, periods, genres, identities, relations, or evaluation
criteria were constructed. Distinguish actors' categories from analytical
categories. Record disagreement, ambiguity, multilingual variation, and revision
history rather than forcing false consensus.

### 4. Construction and quality

Make collection, transcription, OCR, cleaning, normalisation, translation,
deduplication, segmentation, annotation, adjudication, and versioning auditable.
Quality control should preserve meaningful textual or material variation.

### 5. Validation and interpretive validity

Choose validation suited to the resource: expert review, inter-annotator analysis,
gold-standard comparison, source collation, error taxonomy, user study, case-based
validation, or downstream task evaluation. Agreement metrics alone do not prove
that categories are historically or conceptually valid.

### 6. Scholarly use and findings

Demonstrate what the resource enables through representative research questions,
close readings, historical cases, philosophical distinctions, or carefully bounded
computational analyses. A companion model is optional and must not eclipse the
resource's scholarly purpose.

### 7. Ethics, rights, and community consequences

Address consent, privacy, sensitive identities, sacred or community-owned
materials, colonial collections, copyright, access restrictions, harmful reuse,
and takedown or correction mechanisms.

### 8. Documentation, sustainability, and reuse

Specify metadata, citation, licensing, versioning, preservation, governance,
maintenance, machine-readable formats, code, and limitations. Reproducibility does
not override ethical or legal restrictions.

## Evaluation questions

- Does the resource's scope match the claims made from it?
- Can users trace each item and transformation back to a source?
- Are categories defensible across language, period, and community?
- Are uncertainty and disagreement represented?
- Does evaluation test scholarly usefulness as well as technical quality?
- Are missingness and bias analysed rather than listed perfunctorily?
- Can future users cite, interpret, correct, and responsibly reuse the resource?
- Would the paper remain valuable without an optional companion model?

## Paper structure

Adapt by resource type. A common structure includes:

1. scholarly problem, users, and contribution;
2. related resources and the specific unmet need;
3. source provenance, scope, and ethics;
4. construction, representation, and annotation;
5. quality control and validation;
6. scholarly use cases or research questions;
7. limitations, bias, rights, and governance;
8. access, documentation, sustainability, and conclusion.

For explicitly machine-learning benchmarks, the legacy technical references under
references/ remain available for construction pipelines, experiments, and
checklists. Use only the relevant reference when planning an actual machine-learning benchmark;
its example layouts and counts are optional, and venue requirements need current verification.

## Output

For a full planning request, select the useful parts below. For a narrower request,
produce that deliverable without a compulsory audit or full template.

Possible components:

1. resource type and applicability judgment;
2. pillar completeness audit;
3. source, transformation, and category ledger;
4. validation and scholarly-use plan;
5. ethics and sustainability audit;
6. adaptable section skeleton;
7. CRITICAL and MAJOR risks;
8. bounded next actions.

CRITICAL risks include untraceable provenance, fabricated or inaccessible source
claims, severe ethical or rights violations, or a scope-claim mismatch that
invalidates the resource.
