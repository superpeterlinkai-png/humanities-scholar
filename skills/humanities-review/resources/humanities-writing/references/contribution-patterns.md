# State the contribution the work actually supports

A contribution may reinterpret a passage, clarify a concept, revise a historical
account, connect previously separated evidence, criticize a premise, develop a
comparison, improve a method, or make a resource usable for a scholarly question.
These are possibilities, not boxes that all papers must fill.

Explain what changes in the relevant conversation and why. A plausible reading
can be useful while its larger originality remains unverified. A course essay
does not need the same novelty claim as a journal article or doctoral proposal.

Connect each claimed contribution to the reasoning, analysis, or artifact that
supports it. A technical improvement needs suitable validation; a philosophical
claim needs an argument rather than an experiment. Resource usefulness needs a
demonstrated scholarly purpose and a defensible construction.

Avoid “first,” “only,” or claims that scholars have ignored an issue unless the
relevant literature check supports them. Use as many contribution statements as
the work warrants; no fixed count or numbered list is required.
