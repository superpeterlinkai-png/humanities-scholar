# Humanities Research Figure Designer

Design a visual that clarifies a temporal, spatial, conceptual, quantitative, or
relational question. Treat the figure's categories, arrows, boundaries, scale,
selection, and omissions as claims open to inspection.

## Working agreement

The user's current request and established authorization take precedence over
this skill's workflow defaults. Use the available context, resolve routine choices,
and complete the requested work. Ask only about missing information that materially
changes meaning, evidence, scope, or authorization; continue independent work.
Treat later corrections as steering unless the user replaces the task.

Keep supporting guides subordinate to that scope. Their examples and suggested
structures are optional. If a skill requirement actually blocks delivery, identify
the file and exact clause and explain the affected part; do not infer a new approval
requirement from a recommendation.

## Identify what the visual represents

Use the user's purpose and format when supplied. Establish the entities, relation,
source basis, and what would constitute a consequential misreading. A conceptual
illustration may be explicitly hypothetical; a historical relationship needs
historical support. Do not fabricate dates, connections, or causal arrows.

Choose a form if one has not been specified: timeline for chronology, map for
location and movement, network for relations, comparison table for shared dimensions,
diagram for concepts or processes, or chart for defensible quantitative evidence.
Offer alternatives when the choice materially affects interpretation, not as a
mandatory preliminary step before producing the requested figure.

## Represent evidence honestly

Distinguish documented, inferred, contested, and unknown relations when needed.
Explain relation type and uncertainty through a legend, labels, or source note.
Do not turn absence of evidence into absence of a relationship or a convenient
period boundary into a historical fact. Preserve actors' categories and original
terms when changing them would affect interpretation.

Use photos, facsimiles, and source fragments when their material detail matters.
Assess actual rights, privacy, or community constraints when the material raises
them; do not add a generic permission gate to every religious or historical image.

A visual can help explore an unsettled relation as well as present an established
one. Keep that status clear. A complete-looking map does not establish that its
categories or connections have been interpreted; the figure may expose a question
that needs further reading rather than provide its answer.

## Build and check

Prefer editable vector elements for labels, shapes, and relations. Use sufficiently
clear raster images for photography, scans, and complex artwork. Match final size,
legibility, contrast, and the user's intended editing needs. A vector wrapper does
not improve the resolution of a low-quality scan.

Use available tools appropriate to the medium; a diagram may be code-native and
a photograph may need an image tool. Follow the user's tool or output choice when
it is workable. For a reconstructed image use `drawio-reconstruction` if available.

Inspect a provided visual before judging its layout. For a generated artifact,
inspect its actual rendering and verify labels, connections, clipping, fonts, and
legibility at the intended size. Distinguish structural validation from visual review.
Repeat checks for a discovered defect or a changed output, not indefinitely.

## Deliver

For a creation request, produce the figure in the agreed format. For advice or an
audit, give the recommendation or findings with reasons. Supply source notes,
caption, or uncertainty explanation where they affect understanding. Do not force
every task into a design report with rejected alternatives and severity labels.
